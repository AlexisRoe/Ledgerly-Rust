Ownership and lifetimes in depth

Drop, RAII and mem::take

In Rust, the end of a scope is an event you can hook into: whatever a value owns is released right there, in a known order. And when you hold only &mut, mem::take and mem::replace let you move a value out anyway, by leaving another in its place.

By the end of this lesson

You can write an impl Drop guard, predict drop order, avoid the let _ = trap, and move out of a &mut with take / replace.

RAII: resources tied to scopes

"Resource acquisition is initialisation": a value that holds a resource (memory, a file, a lock, a transaction, a timer) releases it in its Drop implementation, and the compiler inserts that call where the owner goes out of scope. That happens on every exit path: normal end, return, ?, and panics.

Drop order

  • Locals drop in reverse order of declaration (last created, first released, like nested finallys).
  • A struct's fields drop in declaration order, after the struct's own drop.
  • drop(value) ends it early: it's just a function that takes ownership and does nothing.
  • The trap: let _ = guard(); drops immediately; _ isn't a variable. let _guard = guard(); keeps it to the end of the scope.

Moving out of a &mut

You can't move a field out of something you only borrow: the owner would be left with a hole. The std::mem functions swap a value in at the same time:

mem::take(&mut x)returns x, leaves Default::default() (empty Vec, "", None…)
mem::replace(&mut x, new)returns x, leaves new

Where the comparison breaks

A GC'd language can't promise when an object goes away, so cleanup has to be explicit (finally, using, close()). Rust's drop is deterministic, which is why Rust APIs rarely have close() methods: you drop the value. The exceptions are async cleanup (drop can't .await) and cleanup that can fail, which need an explicit method such as transaction.commit().await?.
